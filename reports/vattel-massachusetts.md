# The sentences left out: Wolff's rules and Vattel's provisos in Massachusetts, 1764–1775

*Essay, written against the edition vattel-1773 1.19, its reuse tables and divergence notes, and the data reports [Which Vattel did the colonists cite?](?r=vattel-american-sources) and [Vattel in American documents, 1762–1776](?r=vattel-american-reuse). American texts are quoted from A. Bradford (ed.), *Speeches of the Governors of Massachusetts, from 1765 to 1775* (Boston, 1818), H. A. Cushing (ed.), *The Writings of Samuel Adams* (New York, 1904–1906), and *Novanglus, and Massachusettensis* (Boston, 1819).*

Two passages of *Le droit des gens* carried the Massachusetts dispute over the authority of Parliament. Book I § 210 makes a colony part of the state that planted it. Book I § 34 denies legislators the power to change the fundamental laws. They are the most cited passages in the three studies of Vattel's American reception, with eight records each. The rule of each passage is Wolff's. Each passage also has a sentence of Vattel's own that says how the rule applies. Otis's memorial of 1764 printed the proviso of § 210. The House of Representatives and Samuel Adams used the closing sentence of § 34 from 1768 to 1771. The quotations exchanged in 1773 and 1775 give the rules alone. The arguments of Vattel's own sentences went on in the disputants' own words.

Wolff's *Jus gentium* § 292 makes the lands "in quas a Gente occupante coloniæ deductæ sunt" an accessory of that nation's territory, "quantocunque intervallo ab eodem distent". He gives the reason: "Territorium enim constituitur per imperium civile." Vattel's first sentence renders the conclusion and drops the reason: "Lorsqu'une nation s'empare d'un pays éloigné, & y établit une colonie, ce pays, quoique séparé de l'établissement principal, fait naturellement partie de l'état; tout comme ses anciennes possessions" ([I § 210](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#I.210)). His second sentence has no counterpart in Wolff: "Toutes les fois donc que les loix politiques ou les traités n'y apportent point de différence, tout ce qui se dit du territoire d'une nation doit s'entendre aussi de ses colonies." Wolff's colony belongs to the territory because one civil power rules it. Vattel's belongs to it unless laws or treaties provide otherwise.

James Otis's memorial of 1764, which the Massachusetts House voted to send to its agent in London, printed both sentences: "Whenever the political laws or treaties make no distinction between them every thing said of the territory of a nation ought also to extend to its colonies" (1819, 298). It used them to claim for the colonists "all the essential rights of the mother country" (1819, 296). Governor Hutchinson quoted § 210 to the General Court on 6 January 1773 as the principle of "the most celebrated writers upon the law of nations". His quotation ends at "equally with its ancient possessions" (Bradford, 337). He drew the consequence himself: "it is impossible there should be two independent Legislatures in one and the same state" (340). Daniel Leonard, as Massachusettensis, used the same sentence on 9 January 1775. His wording is Hutchinson's, with "equal" for "equally" and "the best writers" for "the most celebrated writers". He added: "Two supreme or independent authorities cannot exist in the same state" (1819, 170). Neither quoted the second sentence. Neither wording has the words "a colony", which stand in the French and in the memorial.

The House answered Hutchinson on 26 January 1773. It referred to "the same author who is quoted by your Excellency", quoted § 34, and went on: "Now the fundamentals of the constitution of this province, are stipulated in the charter" (Bradford, 362). The charter is a political law of the kind that § 210's second sentence names. The House did not cite that sentence. John Adams answered Leonard as Novanglus on 6 March 1775: "We are not told who these 'best writers' are: I think we ought to be introduced to them. But their meaning may be no more, than that it is best they should be incorporated with the ancient establishment by contract, or by some new law and institution" (1819, 80). Adams offered as a guess at the writers' meaning what the second sentence of § 210 says. He did not quote it or name Vattel in that letter.

Section 34 has the same structure. Wolff's *Institutiones* § 984 binds the ruler to the fundamental laws, "nec eas pro lubitu suo mutari posse", and § 1043 concludes "leges fundamentales non subesse potestati legislatoriæ Rectori civitatis competenti". The edition's table finds the same rule in *Jus naturae* VIII. Vattel restates it for a legislative power entrusted "au prince, ou à une assemblée, ou à cette assemblée & au prince conjointement", gives the reason that "c'est de la constitution que ces législateurs tiennent leur pouvoir", and applies it to the King and the two Houses ([I § 34](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#I.34)). His last sentence has no source in the tables: "Mais si le parlement délibéroit de faire un changement si considérable, & que la nation entiere gardât volontairement le silence, elle seroit censée approuver le fait de ses réprésentans." The memorial of 1764 quotes § 34 as far as "submit the constitution itself to their pleasure" and goes on in its own words (1819, 298).

The House used the last sentence four years later. Its letter to the Earl of Shelburne of 15 January 1768, drafted by Samuel Adams, gives it as "the opinion of the greatest writers on the law of nature and nations, that if the parliament should make any considerable change in the constitution, and the nation should be voluntarily silent upon it, this would be considered as an approbation of the act" (Bradford, 140). The sentence is the House's reason for "expressing a deep concern". Its letter to Lord Camden of 29 January takes the next two sections. A change "in its nature delicate and important" is not to be made "without the most pressing reason, or an absolute necessity" ([§ 35](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#I.35)). "When a question arises on the public administration, the nation will judge and determine in conformity to its political constitution" ([§ 36](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#I.36); Cushing, I, 176–177). The tables find no source in Wolff for these sentences. The House's letter to Benjamin Franklin of 29 June 1771 applies the silence clause to "the provincial assemblies" and concludes: "It is therefore our duty to declare our Rights" (Cushing, II, 178). Adams uses it twice more that year as Candidus. The second time he gives it to "a celebrated writer, who thoroughly understood his subject" and goes on to quote Vattel's English example (Cushing, II, 202, 254–255). Vattel's sentence makes silence consent. The House and Adams drew from it a duty to protest.

Both sides argued from silence in 1773. The House's answer of 26 January stopped its quotation of § 34 at "the fundamental laws are excepted from their commission" (Bradford, 362). Hutchinson rested his case on long acceptance: the province's laws "have, in numerous instances, had relation to acts of Parliament", and juries and judges had taken such acts "as part of our rule of law" (Bradford, 337). The House answered that the King's grants of American land by charter, "together with the silence of the nation thereupon, is an evidence that it was an acknowledged prerogative" (355). It closed by regretting that the Governor's speech had left it the choice "either of appearing by our silence to acquiesce in your Excellency's sentiments, or of thus freely discussing this point" (364). The House's closing argument was the one it had taken from Vattel in 1768, now without his words.

The studies of this reception do not separate Wolff's rules from Vattel's sentences. Ossipow and Gerber take the Circular Letter of February 1768 as a silent use of § 34, and Somos calls that reading "equally tenuous" (2021, 212 n. 28). Neither cites the letters of January 1768, which quote § 34 and restate §§ 35–36. Ossipow and Gerber list § 210 in Otis's memorial, Hutchinson's speech and Leonard's letter as one argument to which "Vattel would assent": the colonies as appendages subject to Parliament (2017, 534). The memorial quoted § 210 with the proviso, for the colonists' rights. Somos sees the House and the Governor citing the same passage to opposite ends (2021, 216). Neither notes that the Governor's quotation stops where the memorial's had continued, or that it stops at the end of the sentence Vattel took from Wolff. Ossipow and Gerber also make Locke's chapter "Of the Extent of the Legislative Power" the model of § 34: "There is no doubt that it served as a model for Vattel in this matter" (534). That chapter calls the establishing of the legislative "the first and fundamental positive law of all commonwealths" (*Second Treatise* § 134) and forbids the legislative to transfer its power (§ 141). It has no general rule on fundamental laws. The general rule and its term are Wolff's. Vattel's example of the two Houses giving the king "l'empire plein & absolu" is the case of Locke's § 141.

The American writers did not prefer Wolff's Vattel or avoid it. Of the 42 §§ cited in the three studies, 24 have a close match in Wolff, as the books they come from would give at random. A search of thirty volumes of American writings finds thirteen reuses that the studies do not report. None of them renders a passage that Vattel took closely from Wolff. The American arguments from the law between nations are Wolff's: non-interference in another state's government ([II § 54](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#II.54), *Jus gentium* § 256) and the rules of interpretation the House used in 1773 ([II § 265](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#II.265), *Jus gentium* § 375). Four further patriot arguments rest on §§ where Vattel leaves Wolff or follows another source. On conquest, [III § 201](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#III.201) takes its examples from Grotius III.15.12 and Barbeyrac's notes to it, and opposes Grotius III.8.1 and Wolff's *Institutiones* § 1205, where the victor may change the form of the state at will. On emigration, [I § 220](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#I.220) and [§ 223](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#I.223) oppose Wolff's *Institutiones* § 1019 and *Jus gentium* §§ 153–154, where leaving needs the ruler's consent or a grant. On a people abandoned by its sovereign, [I §§ 201–202](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#I.201) follow Pufendorf VIII.5.9. On two states under one prince, [I § 9](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#I.9) has its parallels in Grotius I.3.7 and Pufendorf VII.5.17. In the three studies the loyalist citations of Vattel are Hutchinson's and Leonard's. Both are of the first sentence of § 210.

The first English translation (London, 1760) prints both sentences of § 210 in the words of Otis's memorial: "antient possessions", "ought also to extend to its colonies". Hutchinson's quotation leaves out the second sentence and the words "a colony". The omission was his. The same translation gives the words of the memorial and of the House for § 34: "It is here demanded whether, if their power extends so far as to the fundamental laws", and "the constitution of the state ought to be fixed". Somos's remark that the memorial was "not using the 1760 English translation" (2021, 210) does not hold. The House's letters of 1768 and Samuel Adams's essays of 1771 and 1772 also follow the 1760 wording.
