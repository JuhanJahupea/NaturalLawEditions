# Five findings on Vattel's "Essai sur le fondement du droit naturel"

*Findings, written against the editions vattel-essai-1747 1.2, burlamaqui-1747 1.14 and vattel-1773 1.19. Each is a claim that the collection's reuse rows and divergence notes support, set against the literature. The rows are machine judgements with a second reading; the links open the passages.*

## 1. Vattel takes his Cicero from the page of Barbeyrac he is attacking

Essai [§ 30](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.30) and [§ 33](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.33) answer the separation of the honest from the useful with Cicero (*De officiis* II.3 and III.3) and Horace ("Atque ipsa utilitas, justi prope mater & æqui"). All three quotations stand on one page, Pufendorf's DNG II.3.10 in Barbeyrac's translation, in Barbeyrac's notes 1 and 3. On the same page note 6 holds that to make utility, "même la plus reelle & la plus universelle", the foundation of moral honesty and of obligation "c'est brouiller les idées". Vattel quotes note 6 word for word in § 30 and answers it with the note's own Cicero. Otsing traces the Ciceronian theme of § 30 to Leibniz, "but perhaps also Burlamaqui and Gentili" (2024, 1377 n. 77). The rows point to Barbeyrac's apparatus, which Burlamaqui uses as well: [*Principes* II.8.4](../viewer/?ed=burlamaqui-1747#P2.8.4) copies the French of DNG II.3.10 and gives the same two Latin passages.

## 2. The word-for-word Wolff stops at PPU I § 266

Otsing calls Vattel's account of obligation "largely a word-for-word selection of ideas from Wolff" (2024, 1376 n. 65) and finds that "utility in terms of perfection is the first principle of moral obligation" (1370). The rows confirm the first point and locate the exception to it. The Essai has 64 match rows with the PPU, 22 of them translations, and Vattel cites §§ 118, 119, 132 and 137. But in § 266 Wolff grants Carneades that all right is useful and denies that utility is its source, since the law of nature "obligat alteri haud raro absque utilitate nostra". Vattel keeps the concession and drops the denial ([§ 25](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.25), [§ 27](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.27)), and he cites none of §§ 263–266. He can do so because [§ 21](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.21) gives utility the content Wolff reserves for the true good. The first principle of the Essai is thus the one point of the PPU's theory of obligation that Vattel does not translate.

## 3. Vattel reads Barbeyrac's voluntarism in the notes, not only in the reply to Leibniz

Hochstrasser treats Barbeyrac's 1718 reply to Leibniz, which concedes that obligation must come "from the will of a superior, and the will of God in particular", as an "inconsistency on this occasion" in an otherwise rationalist theory (1993, 306–307). Vattel reads it as Barbeyrac's settled view. [§ 18](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.18) begins "Mr. Barbeyrac, dans ses notes sur Grotius & sur Puffendorf", and the rows bear this out: 19 match rows from note 4 to DGP I.1.10 and 13 from note 4 to DNG I.6.5, against 48 from the *Réflexions*. This agrees with Grewal, who finds in the notes to Grotius that "the duty of obedience to it can only come from the command of a divine superior" (2024, 29 n. 54). Otsing documents Barbeyrac's position from the 1718 *Devoirs* and a letter to Bourguet (2024, 1369, 1376 nn. 54–55); the two notes are the printed texts Vattel himself names.

## 4. Burlamaqui's answer to Barbeyrac is new in 1747

Otsing names Burlamaqui as the Geneva teacher through whom Vattel probably met these authors (2024, 1365), and Korkman notes that Burlamaqui's treatise "emerged from his lectures at the academy of Geneva" (2006, 259). The rows divide Burlamaqui's contacts with the Essai into two kinds. The definition of obligation as motives that bind the will ([*Principes* I.6.9](../viewer/?ed=burlamaqui-1747#P1.6.9)) has a counterpart in the lecture course printed as the *Élémens*. The passages that answer Barbeyrac have none: [I.6.12–13](../viewer/?ed=burlamaqui-1747#P1.6.12), whose margin reads "Sentiment de Mr. Barbeyrac sur le même sujet", [II.7.9–15](../viewer/?ed=burlamaqui-1747#P2.7.9) and [II.8.4](../viewer/?ed=burlamaqui-1747#P2.8.4). Of the 36 match rows between the two books, 22 are shared arguments and one is a verbatim quotation (the Cicero of finding 1). At the decisive point the answers differ, since Burlamaqui lets reason impose obligation, while Vattel lets it only perceive the connection of motive and action ([§ 40](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.40)). The two replies to Barbeyrac look like parallel work of the 1740s, not lecture material passed from teacher to pupil.

## 5. The note of 1773 that restates the Essai reads like a digest of Burlamaqui

The long note to [Préliminaires § 6](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#Prelim.6), first printed in 1773, gives the Essai's doctrine its one place in the *Droit des gens*: "C'est par le seul desir de ce bonheur que l'on peut lier un être pensant, former les nœuds de l'obligation". Otsing takes it to be "based on Vattel's drafts" (2024, 1376 n. 57). Its wording sits closer to Burlamaqui's *Principes* than to the Essai. The edition finds six parallels with Burlamaqui (I.1.1, I.5.4, I.5.5, I.6.9, II.2.3, II.5.7), among them "La grande fin de tout être doué d'intelligence & de sentiment" against Burlamaqui's "Etres doués de connoissance & de sentiment", and only one weak parallel with the Essai. The note itself says that its sketch is "développée dans plus d'un ouvrage estimé", and the additions of 1773 include notes by the editors, one of which cites a journal of 1771. Whether Vattel's drafts summarised Burlamaqui, or the editors shaped the note, is a question the manuscripts could settle.
