# Whose foundations? The note of 1773 to the Préliminaires of *Le droit des gens*

*Essay, written against the edition vattel-1773 1.19 and its layer of 1773 changes, with the editions vattel-essai-1747 1.2 and burlamaqui-1747 1.14. Quotations from the 1773 edition were checked against its transcription and the Munich scans.*

The only statement of the foundations of natural law in Vattel's *Droit des gens* is a long note to Préliminaires § 6 that first appeared in the posthumous Neuchâtel edition of 1773: "La grande fin de tout être doué d'intelligence & de sentiment est le bonheur. C'est par le seul desir de ce bonheur que l'on peut lier un être pensant, former les nœuds de l'obligation qui doit le soumettre à quelque regle" ([Prélim. § 6, note](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#Prelim.6)). Scholars read the note as Vattel's own summary of his doctrine. It should not be read that way. The 1773 edition says that its notes were made by others from materials Vattel left unfinished, one of them was written after his death, and the note to § 6 differs from the other additions in kind and from Vattel's own writings in its vocabulary, which is that of Burlamaqui's *Principes du droit naturel*. The note shows how Vattel's foundations were presented to readers in Neuchâtel in 1773; it is not evidence of what Vattel held.

The note has been read as Vattel's since the first English version of it. The translator of 1797 set out "to add the author's valuable notes from the posthumous edition printed at Neuchatel in 1773", and Kapossy and Whatmore, whose edition reprints that translation, describe the notes added in 1773 as "intended by Vattel for a second edition he did not live to complete" (2008, xxi). Brühlmeier calls the note to § 6 "an important and remarkable footnote" that summarises "the specific Vattelian, neo-Leibnizian understanding of natural law" (1995, 68). Haggenmacher knows it is posthumous, but supposes that Vattel felt the need, "au cours d'une relecture, d'ajouter tout au moins une sommaire esquisse du droit naturel" (2011, 14), and the note has been cited for Vattel's view of God's will and obligation (Otsing 2024, 1376 n. 57). Of the studies I have read, only Chetail doubts an addition of 1773, and then because of the edition's careless printing; he also states, without a source, that the edition was made from a manuscript with additions "de la main même de l'auteur" (2011, 407 n. 109).

The edition of 1773 gives a different account of its notes. Its "Abrégé de la vie de M. de Vattel", which Ieva attributes to Frédéric-Samuel Ostervald, the director of the Société typographique (2019, 54–55), says that Vattel had meant to revise the work "& à l'enrichir de quelques notes dont les matériaux ont été trouvés dans ses manuscrits, & auxquelles de nombreuses occupations & une mort prématurée ne lui ont pas permis de mettre lui-même la derniere main. On les a recueillies avec le plus grand soin dans cette édition" (II, iii). By this account Vattel left materials, not notes, and the notes were made from them by the editors. The title page says only "Nouvelle édition augmentée", and nothing in the text distinguishes the editors' work from the author's.

The collation of the editions of 1758 and 1773 shows what these materials were. The edition's layer of changes counts 59 additions to the notes. Nearly all of them are excerpts, quotations and historical examples from Grotius's *Annales*, de Witt's letters, Velly, Mariana, Xenophon or the *Histoire de Timur-bec*, the kind of matter a reader copies out for later use. Placing them was the editors' work, and they did not always place them well: the same passage of de Witt on reprisals stands at [II § 348](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#II.348) and again at [III § 142](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#III.142), where the translator of 1797 found it "very much out of place" (Kapossy and Whatmore 2008, 545).

One addition cannot be Vattel's. The note to [IV § 110](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#IV.110) reads: "Il n'y a pas long-tems qu'on a vu un ministre étranger en France, poursuivi par ses créanciers & à qui la cour de France réfusa un passeport. Voyez Journal politique de Bouillon du 1 février 1771". Vattel died at Neuchâtel in December 1767. The note speaks in the author's present about an event of three years later, so the editors wrote in his voice and did not say so. Trampus uses this note to show that a Bern manuscript copies the edition of 1773 (2019, 181–182) but does not remark on its date, and the modern edition marks it, like every other addition, "Note added in 1773/1797 editions".

The note to § 6 is the other exception, and it is different in kind. It is the only addition that sets out a doctrine instead of quoting a source, and it says whom it is for: "en faveur de ceux qui n'ont point fait de ce droit une étude systématique, il ne sera pas hors de propos d'en donner ici une idée générale". It ends by sending the reader to "plus d'un ouvrage estimé" in which this general idea is developed. A summary of this kind is what a publisher adds to make a treatise self-contained for a wider public, and it is unlike the excerpts found among the author's papers.

Its language is not Vattel's. In the "Essai sur le fondement du droit naturel" (1747), where Vattel states his own foundations, active obligation is "la connexion du motif avec l'action" ([§ 12](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.12)) and the first principle of all obligation is "ce bien cette utilité" ([§ 22](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.22)); the "Essai" uses *utilité* 35 times and *motif* 43 times in some ten thousand words. The note, of about six hundred words, uses neither. Instead it says *bonheur* seven times and *grande fin* four times, an expression the "Essai" never uses, and none of its formulas occurs in the *Questions de droit naturel* (1762), Vattel's last work on these matters. Its sequence is that of Burlamaqui's *Principes* of 1747: happiness as man's end, a first truth known "par le sentiment intérieur" ([I.5.4](../viewer/?ed=burlamaqui-1747#P1.5.4)), the providence that extends to "tous les Etres doués de connoissance & de sentiment" ([I.5.5](../viewer/?ed=burlamaqui-1747#P1.5.5)), obligation as motives that bind the will "comme autant de liens" ([I.6.9](../viewer/?ed=burlamaqui-1747#P1.6.9)), a God who wills the happiness of his creatures "autant que leur nature ... les en rend capables" ([II.2.3](../viewer/?ed=burlamaqui-1747#P2.2.3)), and reason and God's authority concurring in one obligation ([II.5.7](../viewer/?ed=burlamaqui-1747#P2.5.7)). The parallels are of doctrine and order rather than of wording, and the note also borrows an idiom of the *Droit des gens*, "l'ennemi du genre humain". It reads as a digest of the Genevan textbook, written in a style close enough to Vattel's to pass for his.

Three consequences follow. First, *Le droit des gens* as Vattel published it contains no statement of the foundations of natural law, so Haggenmacher's observation that natural law is "en somme présupposé" in the work (2011, 14) holds without the qualification the note seemed to supply. Second, accounts of Vattel's foundations should rest on the "Essai" and the *Questions*, as most specialist accounts in fact do; where the note departs from them, it is not evidence of Vattel's view. It does depart on a point that has carried weight: the note puts God's will and "la simple indication de la nature" on a par, as two sources that "se réunissent à former la méme obligation", whereas the "Essai" had made the will of a superior only "un principe prochain" beneath utility ([§ 29](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.29)), and it is the note's sentence that grounds Brühlmeier's "specifically Leibnizian point" (1995, 68 n. 49). Third, the additions of 1773 form a layer of mixed authorship which the English translation and its modern reprint present as Vattel's, and which has to be edited as such, note by note.

The question could be settled from the archive of the Société typographique de Neuchâtel. Letters between Ostervald and the Vattel family, or the copy sent to the printer, would show which notes were found among Vattel's papers and which were written in the shop. Until then the note to § 6 is best cited as the edition's, not the author's.
