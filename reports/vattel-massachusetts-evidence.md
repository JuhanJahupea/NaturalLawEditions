# Vattel in Massachusetts, 1764–1776: what the texts show

*Essay, written against the edition vattel-1773 1.19 and the data reports [Vattel in American documents, 1762–1776](?r=vattel-american-reuse) and [Which Vattel did the colonists cite?](?r=vattel-american-sources). The 1760 English is the University of Michigan copy (Google Books MRo2AQAAMAAJ). American texts are cited from A. Bradford (ed.), *Speeches of the Governors of Massachusetts* (1818), H. A. Cushing (ed.), *The Writings of Samuel Adams* (1904–1906), and *Novanglus, and Massachusettensis* (1819).*

Thirty volumes of American writing from 1762 to 1776 were searched against Vattel's *Law of Nations*. They hold the pamphlets of Otis, Thacher, Dulany, Bland, Dickinson, Jefferson, Wilson, Hamilton, Seabury and Paine, the Massachusetts state papers of 1765–1775, the writings of Samuel Adams and John Adams to 1776, and the *Journals of the Continental Congress* for 1774–1776. The search finds 29 reuses of Vattel in 18 documents. Thirteen are not in Ossipow and Gerber, Somos or Schmeeckle.

## Place and time

Twenty-seven of the 29 reuses are Massachusetts texts. The other two are in Bland's *Inquiry* (Williamsburg, 1766). All but two fall between 1764 and 1773. The two later ones are Leonard's and John Adams's use of I § 210 in 1775. The journals of Congress, *Common Sense*, Jefferson's *Summary View*, Wilson, Dickinson, Hamilton, Dulany and Seabury have none. Nine of the 18 documents are by Samuel Adams or by the House in his drafting.

## Book I

Twenty-one of the 29 reuses come from Book I, on the constitution of a state. Book II gives only rules of interpretation: John Adams copied three into his diary in December 1765, when the courts were closed over the Stamp Act, and the House read the charter by two more in 1773. Book III gives the limits of a conqueror's right ([III § 201](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#III.201)) and the line between patience and resistance ([III § 290](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#III.290)).

[I § 34](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#I.34) is used eight times: in Otis's memorial of 1764, the House's letters to Shelburne (1768) and Franklin (1771), three essays of Samuel Adams (1771–1772), and the House's two answers to Hutchinson (1773). It holds that legislators cannot change the fundamental laws, "For the constitution of the state ought to be fixed: and since that was first established by the nation, which afterwards trusted certain persons with the legislative power, the fundamental laws are excepted from their commission." The House applied it to the province: "Now the fundamentals of the constitution of this province, are stipulated in the charter" (Bradford, 362).

## Silence

The last sentence of § 34 reads, in the English of 1760: "But if the parliament entered into a debate on making so considerable a change, and the whole nation was voluntarily silent upon it, this would be considered as an approbation of the act of its representatives." It has no source in Wolff or elsewhere in the edition's tables.

The House gave it to Shelburne on 15 January 1768 as "the opinion of the greatest writers on the law of nature and nations" (Bradford, 140). It wrote to Franklin on 29 June 1771: "If ever the provincial assemblies should be voluntarily silent ... it might be considered as an approbation of it, or at least a tacit consent ... It is therefore our duty to declare our Rights" (Cushing, II, 178). Samuel Adams, as Candidus, wrote in August 1771: "If therefore we are voluntarily silent while the single duty on tea is continued ... we are in extreme hazard" (II, 202). In October he quoted "a celebrated writer, who thoroughly understood his subject": "if we are voluntarily silent ... it will be consider'd as an approbation of the change" (II, 254). The House closed its answer of 26 January 1773 by regretting that the Governor had left it the choice "either of appearing by our silence to acquiesce in your Excellency's sentiments, or of thus freely discussing this point" (Bradford, 364).

Both sides took acquiescence as binding. Hutchinson argued that the province's laws had long "had relation to acts of Parliament" (Bradford, 337). The House answered that the King's charters, "together with the silence of the nation thereupon", proved his prerogative (355). John Adams granted that the colonies had accepted the acts of trade "by our implied consent, by long usage and uninterrupted acquiescence" (1819, 89).

## Names

Vattel is named in 12 of the 29 reuses: in Otis's and Thacher's pamphlets, Bland's footnote, John Adams's diary, two essays of Samuel Adams and the Boston report of 1772. He is named in none of the nine reuses in official papers. There he is "the greatest writers on the law of nature and nations" (the House, 1768), "the most celebrated writers upon the law of nations" (Hutchinson, 1773), "the celebrated author, whom your Excellency has quoted" (the House, 1773) and "a learned writer on the laws of nature and nations ... that great man" (the House, 2 March 1773; Cushing, II, 452).

## The English of 1760

Each quotation and paraphrase was compared with the London translation of 1760 and with the text of 1797. Sixteen of 28 are nearer 1760. Two are nearer 1797, both because of OCR faults in the 1760 copy. No American text follows a reading peculiar to 1797. Otis's memorial and the House give § 34 in the 1760 words "It is here demanded whether, if their power extends so far" and "ought to be fixed" (1797: "It is asked whether", "ought to possess stability"). The House's "hath a right to interpret at pleasure" (1773) is the 1760 "at his pleasure" (1797: "according to his own fancy"). Somos's remark that the memorial of 1764 was "not using the 1760 English translation" (2021, 210) does not hold.

## Wolff

No document names Wolff. Eleven reuses render passages that Vattel took closely from Wolff: the first sentence of [I § 210](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#I.210) (*Jus gentium* § 292), the rule of § 34 (*Institutiones* §§ 984, 1043; *Jus naturae* VIII), and the rules of interpretation (*Jus gentium* § 375; *Institutiones* §§ 804, 813). All eleven are among the uses the studies already report. Hutchinson and Leonard, the only loyalists in the record, cite the Wolffian sentence of § 210 and leave out Vattel's proviso, which the 1760 English prints: "Whenever therefore the political laws, or treaties, make no distinction between them, every thing said of the territory of a nation, ought also to extend to its colonies." None of the thirteen newly found reuses renders a close passage of Wolff. They include the silence clause, the vigilance against "silent and slow attacks" ([I § 30](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#I.30)), the praise of the English constitution ([I § 24](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#I.24)), the caution on great changes and the nation as judge of its constitution ([I §§ 35–36](../viewer/?ed=vattel-1773#I.35)) and III § 290. The rest are loose paraphrases of Wolff's definition of the state (I §§ 1–2) and a rule of interpretation with no source in the tables (II § 283). Close Wolff matches cover 54 per cent of the sections of the *Droit des gens* and 11 of the 29 American reuses.

## The Declaration

Neither Jefferson's draft nor the adopted text shares wording with Vattel. The nearest sections reach a cosine of 0.11 to 0.14; the quotations above reach 0.25 to 0.55. One sentence joins Vattel to Locke. "Mankind are more disposed to suffer, while evils are sufferable, than to right themselves by abolishing the forms to which they are accustomed" takes Locke's "the people, who are more disposed to suffer than right themselves by resistance, are not apt to stir" (*Second Treatise* § 230) and adds "while evils are sufferable". The 1760 English of III § 290 reads: "Every citizen should even patiently suffer supportable evils, rather than disturb the public peace." Bland had paraphrased it in 1766: "to suffer patiently rather than disturb the publick Peace". Franklin wrote to Dumas in December 1775 that Vattel's book had been "continually in the hands of the members of our Congress". The journals show no trace of it.

## Conclusion

In these texts Vattel is a Massachusetts author of the years 1764 to 1773, read in the London English of 1760. Boston used him for the constitution of a state: a constitution set by the nation and placed beyond its legislators, with the charter standing as that constitution. Both sides cited the rules Vattel took from Wolff. The patriots argued from the sentences he wrote himself, and above all from the one that makes silence consent. They drew from it a duty to declare their rights, and they declared them in 1768, 1771, 1772 and 1773. In their official papers they gave his sentences as the law of nature and nations, without his name. The textual record ends before independence. The Declaration keeps one clause of Vattel's inside a sentence of Locke's.
