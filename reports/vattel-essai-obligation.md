# Obligation without a superior: Vattel's *Essai* between Wolff and Barbeyrac

*Essay, written against the editions vattel-essai-1747 1.2 and burlamaqui-1747 1.14: their reuse rows, divergence notes and audits. A test of the collection's essays: the claims rest on the edition's text, which was checked for every quotation; the reuse rows and divergence notes are machine judgements with a second reading.*

Vattel's "Essai sur le fondement du droit naturel" (1747) answers Barbeyrac with Wolff's definition of obligation, but it grounds that obligation in a principle Wolff had refused, the agent's own utility. The edition's reuse rows show the borrowing, since Vattel's definitions of active and passive obligation translate Wolff's PPU. Its divergence notes show the departure: on the question whether utility is the source of right, Vattel keeps Wolff's concession and drops Wolff's denial.

The quarrel Vattel entered was a generation old. Leibniz wrote his judgement on Pufendorf's *De officio* in 1706, objecting to an obligation grounded in a superior's will, and Barbeyrac printed it in French, with his own *Réflexions du traducteur*, in the fourth edition of the *Devoirs de l'homme et du citoyen* (1718). The "Essai" quotes that edition by page. In the *Réflexions* Barbeyrac held that the reason why men are obliged to follow the rules of justice, "ce qui leur impose la nécessité morale de s'y conformer, c'est la volonté de DIEU, qui en qualité de leur Maître Souverain a plein droit de gêner leur Liberté" (§ 15). On the exchange itself see Schneewind (1996) and Korkman (2003); on Barbeyrac's natural law as a whole, Hochstrasser (1993). Vattel notes that part of the "Essai" was written in 1742 ([§ 29, note 1](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.29)).

Vattel's definition of obligation is Wolff's. [§ 11](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.11) asks what men mean when they say they are obliged, and answers that only motives, "apperçus & pesés par la raison", can determine a free being against its inclination: "Comment sommes-nous donc obligés à faire une certaine action ? C'est par la liaison d'un motif puissant avec cette même action." [§ 12](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.12) then gives Wolff's pair, active obligation as "la connexion du motif avec l'action" and passive obligation as "une nécessité morale d'agir ou de ne pas agir", with a reference to PPU I § 118, and [§ 13](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.13) derives the second from the first, citing § 119. The edition holds 64 match rows between the "Essai" and the PPU, 22 of them translations, and 22 more with the *Theologia naturalis*, whose § 973 states the same definition.

This definition lets Vattel accept the voluntarists' formulas while denying their ground. He quotes Pufendorf's definition of obligation as "une qualité morale en vertu de laquelle on est astraint par une nécessité morale" (DNG I.1.21) and accepts it as a definition of passive obligation, objecting only that Pufendorf "ne nous dit point en quoi consiste cette qualité morale" ([§ 14](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.14)). Against Barbeyrac, who "en disant qu'il n'y a point d'obligation sans supérieur" had made obligation a dependence on a superior's will, he asks why that dependence binds us ([§ 18](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.18)). Every answer Barbeyrac could give names a motive (the justice of obedience, or the superior's power to punish), so that he "retombe dans notre définition". Pufendorf had required a superior with both the force to punish and just reasons for commanding (DNG I.6.9); Vattel turns each into a motive and drops the requirement that both concur. In [§ 29](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.29) he even offers Barbeyrac a settlement: the will of a superior is not the first principle of obligation, "mais elle en fait sans doute un principe prochain très-solide".

The departure from Wolff comes with the first principle. Having made utility "le motif le plus général, le motif primitif qui nous détermine", Vattel concludes in [§ 22](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.22) that "ce bien cette utilité, est le prémier principe de toute obligation". Wolff had refused this step. In PPU I § 266 he grants Carneades that all right is useful, but denies that utility is the source of right, and adds that the law of nature "obligat alteri haud raro absque utilitate nostra", as in giving alms. Vattel keeps the concession and drops the denial. In [§ 25](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.25) men enter society for their utility and must sacrifice "un avantage présent, comme à un plus grand bien" to its laws: "Voilà le vrai principe de l'obligation où nous sommes de les observer". The divergence pass found this departure twice, at § 25 for civil laws and at § 27 for obligation in general. Vattel cites PPU I §§ 118, 119, 132 and 137 in the "Essai", but not § 266.

Vattel can keep Wolff's definition and abandon his denial because he redefines utility. Wolff's *utile* is whatever serves some use of ours (PPU I § 262). Vattel's is whatever "peut véritablement contribuer à la perfection de notre Ame, à celle de notre Corps & à notre bien-être dans ce monde", above all of the soul: "Voilà la vraie Définition de l'utile" ([§ 21](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.21)). This is close to what Wolff calls the true good, and it lets the argument against Barbeyrac close in [§ 27](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.27). Asked why one must avoid a bad action that brings no punishment, Vattel finds no answer except that it is contrary to the order and perfection of one's being: "Et cela revient à mon sentiment, qui fonde l'obligation sur l'utilité; car il m'est sans doute utile d'être parfait." The note to § 12 makes a similar shift. Wolff measured moral necessity by the rectitude of the action (PPU I § 115), while Vattel defines as morally necessary what "ne peut pas manquer d'arriver, vû la nature de l'être intelligent qui la produit", on the model of physical necessity. The Wolffian definition is kept, and its ground moves from the rectitude of actions to the good of the agent.

The second half of the "Essai" is a reply to the *Réflexions*, quoted at length. The edition holds 65 match rows with the *Devoirs* volume: 48 from Barbeyrac's *Réflexions* and 15 from Leibniz's *Jugement*, among them 25 verbatim quotations and 24 open disagreements. Most of these are counter-arguments, and the divergence layer counts them differently, since it records only the places where two texts answer the same question differently and keeps one note for each. It keeps 18 notes against Barbeyrac, and in §§ 35–40 they come down to four questions: whether there is a middle between obligation and its absence (§ 35), whether the nature of things can oblige by itself (§ 36), whether reason alone can bind us against the passions (§ 37), and whether an obligation known by reason can cease at our will (§ 40).

The last of these shows how the definition carries the reply. Barbeyrac argued that reason, considered apart from its Creator, "n'est au fond autre chose, que nous-mêmes", and that "personne ne peut s'imposer à soi même une nécessité indispensable d'agir". Vattel calls this "une pure pétition de principe" and answers that "ce n'est pas notre raison qui nous impose l'obligation, elle ne fait qu'apperçevoir la liaison du motif avec l'action" ([§ 40](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.40)). Since the connection of motive and action lies in the nature of things, it cannot cease at our will. Burlamaqui met the same objection in the *Principes du droit naturel*, printed at Geneva the same year, and also found that it "suppose ce qui est en question". But he answered that reason itself imposes the obligation, and that a man can escape it "à ses périls & risques" ([Principes II.7.11](../viewer/?ed=burlamaqui-1747#P2.7.11)). The two replies share the diagnosis and differ on what imposes the obligation, which is the point Vattel's definition was built to settle. Whether Vattel knew Burlamaqui's Geneva lectures is open.

The atheist marks the practical limit of this theory. Wolff held that the atheist admits the law of nature as far as what man owes to himself and to other men, and loses the duties towards God (PPU I § 255). In [§ 34](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.34) Vattel defends Leibniz's remark that to neglect the consideration of another life "c'est détruire plusieurs devoirs de la vie", but not all of them, because many duties have motives "dans leur utilité, même par rapport à cette vie". The line between lost and kept duties is drawn by the presence of a motive, so that duties to others which demand a sacrifice can fall with the afterlife. Wolff's line, which runs between duties to God and duties to men, does not follow from Vattel's definition.

The "Essai" ends with Leibniz and not with Vattel's own words. [§ 41](../viewer/?ed=vattel-essai-1747#E.41) quotes with approval the paragraph of the *Jugement* in which those who do good from love, and not from hope or fear of a superior, "imitent en quelque maniere la justice de Dieu" and need no "commandement d'un Supérieur". Barbeyrac had answered this paragraph in *Réflexions* § 16, holding that the man who overcomes a bad temperament is the more just; Vattel passes over the reply. The quotation also concedes the limit of the theory, since those who have not reached this perfection "ne sont susceptibles d'obligation que par l'espérance ou la crainte". For these readers the superior's sanction remains the decisive motive, and Vattel's definition keeps a place for it.

The reuse rows and divergence notes come from model readers with a second reading ([audit](../audit/README.md)); the divergence layer only compares Vattel with the sources the collection holds, so his Swiss and Leibnizian contexts outside these texts do not appear in it. The issue traced here, the ground of obligation, also runs through Burlamaqui and the *Droit des gens*; the collection's [genealogy of that issue](../issues/?i=ground-of-obligation) follows it from text to text.
